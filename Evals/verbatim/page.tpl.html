How this was measured

Audio was fed in 320 ms chunks to each recogniser’s streaming API, exactly as the app feeds a live microphone. Delay is the gap between a word’s audio ending and the recogniser committing to that word — the same definition Evals/recognizer/latency.swift uses, so the numbers sit alongside the existing Apple measurements. Speed is audio seconds per wall-clock second on an M5 Pro.

Vosk is credited with its partial hypotheses, not only its finals. Scoring it on finals alone put its mean delay at 6.2s, which would have libelled it: a final only lands at an endpoint, while the partial is what a dictation panel would actually display. On partials it lands at 0.58s.

What the reference is, and is not

There is no hand-checked ground truth for these 28 recordings, so the reference is Apple’s own transcript from the session log. The WER vs Apple column therefore measures distance from the incumbent, not accuracy — a model could beat Apple and score worse here. The two columns the decision actually rests on, marks per 100 words and spoken marks kept, are counted from each model’s own output and do not depend on the reference being right.

“Spoken kept” above 60 means the model preserved more spoken punctuation words than Apple did on the same audio — direct evidence that Apple is silently converting them. Whisper keeps the most of all, at 113, and is still disqualified: it does not stream, and it invents more punctuation than Apple does.

Why Amazon is absent

StartStreamTranscription exposes parameters for vocabulary, language identification, PII redaction, diarisation and partial-result stabilisation — and nothing for punctuation, formatting, or inverse text normalisation. Punctuation is always on and cannot be disabled, so Transcribe streaming cannot produce a verbatim transcript at any setting.
